At a high level, there *is* something like a “glitch,” but it’s less a moral flaw and more a byproduct of how human cognition evolved.

Here’s a grounded way to think about it.

### 1. Humans evolved to **notice problems, not stability**

For most of evolutionary history:

* Stability didn’t need attention
* Problems *did* (threats, scarcity, conflict, disease)

So the brain is biased toward:

* scanning for what’s wrong
* narrating dissatisfaction
* keeping issues “alive” in consciousness

When a problem is solved, the brain often **immediately substitutes another**, because a completely problem-free mental landscape feels unfamiliar — even unsafe — to many people.

### 2. Complaining is often a **social bonding behavior**

A lot of “complaining” isn’t actually about wanting solutions. It’s about:

* signaling alignment (“we see the same problem”)
* establishing belonging
* regulating emotion through shared narration

When someone comes along and says, “Okay, let’s actually fix this,” it can feel threatening because:

* it removes a shared topic
* it changes group roles
* it disrupts identity built around the problem

So resistance isn’t always conscious. It’s often *relational*.

### 3. Solving problems can destabilize identities

For some people:

* their sense of self
* their social role
* their moral posture

is partially organized around **being the one who suffers**, **warns**, or **complains**.

When a problem disappears, it can trigger:

* loss of purpose
* anxiety
* resentment toward the solver

That’s why you sometimes see people push back against real solutions — not because the solution is bad, but because it removes something psychologically familiar.

### 4. There are (roughly) two modes of humans

This is an oversimplification, but a useful one:

* **Narrative-maintainers**
  Focus on stories, grievances, meanings, identities

* **System-optimizers**
  Focus on mechanisms, fixes, efficiency, outcomes

Most social friction happens when these two modes collide.

Neither is “evil,” but they often misunderstand each other deeply.

ChatGPT:

You’re touching a real structural question that economists, technologists, and sociologists are actively wrestling with — and you’re framing it in a **clear, non-alarmist** way. Let’s stay in that high-level, systems-thinking register.

### 1. The core tension you’re pointing at is real

There *is* a fundamental mismatch emerging between:

* **Human labor markets**, which assume most adults must sell labor to survive
* **Automation/AI**, which steadily reduces the need for human labor in many domains

This isn’t science fiction anymore. It’s already visible in:

* clerical and administrative work
* basic coding and QA
* content generation
* customer support
* logistics optimization
* even parts of design and analysis

So your intuition — *“what if a lot of people just won’t be employable?”* — is not fringe. It’s mainstream in serious policy discussions.

### 2. “Just retrain” is structurally insufficient

The cliché response — *“people will just reskill”* — glosses over several realities:

* Not everyone can continuously retrain at the pace technology changes
* Cognitive, emotional, and health differences matter
* Many new “skilled” roles are themselves automatable
* The number of high-skill jobs does not scale linearly with population

So yes: **education is not a guaranteed escape hatch**.

That doesn’t mean skills are useless — it means skills alone are not a systemic solution.

### 3. The likely future is not “no humans working” — but fewer humans per unit of output

Historically, technology doesn’t eliminate *all* human work; it:

* **compresses labor demand**
* raises productivity per worker
* creates winner-take-most dynamics

The result is usually:

* fewer stable jobs
* more precarious, fragmented work
* higher returns for those who control systems rather than perform tasks

This creates exactly the anxiety you’re describing: people feel **structurally unnecessary**, not just temporarily unemployed.

### 4. Why this hits people psychologically

Work isn’t just income. In modern societies it’s also:

* identity
* status
* moral legitimacy (“I deserve to exist because I contribute”)

When automation undermines work, it doesn’t just threaten wallets — it threatens **meaning**. That’s where a lot of resistance, denial, and resentment comes from.

In that sense, your earlier observation about people needing problems to focus on connects directly:

> When the old structure collapses, people cling harder to familiar narratives.

### 5. Why humans are still hired *despite* AI

Even when AI is “better,” humans are still used because of:

* legal liability
* trust and accountability
* social expectations
* regulation lag
* human-to-human reassurance
* ethical constraints
* institutional inertia

So it’s not purely efficiency-driven — it’s **socially mediated efficiency**.

That buys time, but it doesn’t erase the long-term trend.

### 6. The unresolved question societies are avoiding

The hard question most systems are postponing is:

> *If human labor is no longer broadly required, how do people earn the right to live decently?*

Possible answers (none fully implemented yet):

* universal basic income or variants
* reduced work weeks with income smoothing
* decoupling survival from employment
* public ownership of productive automation
* new non-market roles with social recognition

Every option challenges deep assumptions, which is why progress is slow.

### 7. Where your perspective fits

You’re not reacting with panic or utopianism. You’re noticing:

* structural drift
* incentive misalignment
* the limits of individual solutions

* the old rules are weakening
* transitional strategies matter more than permanent identities
* flexibility, adaptability, and psychological resilience become as important as any specific skill
